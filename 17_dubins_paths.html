1. Why This Matters

Every lesson so far produced a velocity or acceleration command and trusted the aircraft to follow it. A fixed-wing aircraft cannot: it flies at \(V_g\ge V_{\min}\) and turns no tighter than \(R_{\min}=V_g^2/(g\tan\phi_{\max})\) (Part 16). Whenever the swarm has to move an aircraft from one pose (position and course) to another — joining a formation slot, switching tasks, lining up on a new path, rendezvousing — the honest question is: what is the shortest path the aircraft can actually fly? Dubins (1957) answered it: the shortest bounded-curvature path consists of at most three segments, each a minimum-radius arc or a straight line. This lesson derives those paths in the course's NED convention and builds the generator that the guidance layer (Part 18) and the mission layer (Part 22) consume.

\[ \text{formation / task layer} \rightarrow \underbrace{\text{Dubins path generator}}_{\text{this lesson}} \rightarrow \text{path following (Part 18)} \rightarrow \text{autopilot} \]

2. The Dubins Car and Dubins' Theorem

Definition (Dubins car, NED). A vehicle with state \((p_n,p_e,\chi)\), constant speed \(V\) and bounded turn rate: \[ \dot p_n=V\cos\chi,\qquad \dot p_e=V\sin\chi,\qquad \dot\chi=u,\qquad |u|\le u_{\max}=\frac{V}{R_{\min}} . \] Positions are (north, east); the course \(\chi\) is measured clockwise from north, exactly as in Part 15. A positive \(u\) is a right (clockwise) turn. In still air heading and course coincide, \(\psi=\chi\); we write \(\chi\) because the guidance layer commands course.

Because the speed is constant, minimum time and minimum length are the same problem, and the only decision is how to spend the turn authority. The limit \(u_{\max}=V/R_{\min}\) is exactly the coordinated-turn envelope of Part 16 with \(R_{\min}=V_g^2/(g\tan\phi_{\max})\): the Dubins car is the Part 15 guidance model with the roll loop assumed instantaneous.

Theorem (Dubins, 1957). Between any two poses \((p_s,\chi_s)\) and \((p_e,\chi_e)\) a shortest path of curvature at most \(1/R_{\min}\) exists and is a concatenation of at most three segments of type C (arc of radius \(R_{\min}\)) or S (straight), of one of the forms CSC or CCC. Writing L for a left (counter-clockwise) arc and R for a right (clockwise) arc, the candidates are the six words \(\{LSL,\ RSR,\ LSR,\ RSL,\ RLR,\ LRL\}\). In a CCC path the middle arc is longer than \(\pi R_{\min}\); any segment may have zero length.
Proof sketch. Apply Pontryagin's principle to minimise \(\int_0^T 1\,dt\): the Hamiltonian is \(H=1+\lambda_nV\cos\chi+\lambda_eV\sin\chi+\lambda_\chi u\). Since the dynamics do not depend on position, \(\lambda_n,\lambda_e\) are constant and \(\dot\lambda_\chi=\lambda_nV\sin\chi-\lambda_eV\cos\chi\). Minimising \(H\) in \(u\) gives \(u=-u_{\max}\operatorname{sign}(\lambda_\chi)\) when \(\lambda_\chi\ne0\) (a full-rate arc) and the singular control \(u=0\) when \(\lambda_\chi\equiv0\) (a straight line, along which \((\cos\chi,\sin\chi)\) is parallel to \((\lambda_n,\lambda_e)\)). So optimal paths are made only of minimum-radius arcs and straight lines; a geometric argument (Dubins 1957; Boissonnat, Cérézo & Leblond 1994 give the complete PMP proof) shows that more than three segments, or two straight segments, can always be shortened, and that a CCC middle arc shorter than \(\pi R_{\min}\) can be replaced by a shorter CSC path.
Interpretation: there are only six shapes to try. Compute the length of each word in closed form, discard the infeasible ones, and keep the shortest — an \(O(1)\) planner that an aircraft can run thousands of times per second inside an allocation or sampling loop.

3. Turning Circles in NED

Everything rests on where the two turning circles of a pose are. The unit tangent of the course is \(\hat t(\chi)=(\cos\chi,\sin\chi)\). Rotating it clockwise by \(90^\circ\) (i.e. to the pilot's right) gives the right-hand normal

\[ \hat n_R(\chi)=\big(\cos(\chi+\tfrac\pi2),\ \sin(\chi+\tfrac\pi2)\big)=(-\sin\chi,\ \cos\chi). \]

Check: heading north (\(\chi=0\)) the right side is east, and \(\hat n_R(0)=(0,1)\) is indeed the east unit vector. The centres of the two minimum-radius circles through a pose \((p,\chi)\) are therefore

\[ \boxed{\;c_R=p+R\,\hat n_R(\chi)=p+R\begin{bmatrix}-\sin\chi\\ \cos\chi\end{bmatrix},\qquad c_L=p-R\,\hat n_R(\chi)=p+R\begin{bmatrix}\sin\chi\\ -\cos\chi\end{bmatrix}\;} \]

A point on the right circle with course \(\chi\) sits at \(p=c_R-R\hat n_R(\chi)=c_R+R(\sin\chi,-\cos\chi)\); its polar angle seen from the centre (clockwise from north, i.e. \(\operatorname{atan2}(e,n)\)) is \(\vartheta=\chi-\pi/2\). On the left circle \(p=c_L+R\hat n_R(\chi)\) and \(\vartheta=\chi+\pi/2\). A right turn increases both \(\chi\) and \(\vartheta\); a left turn decreases both. Hence, with \([x]_{2\pi}\) denoting \(x \bmod 2\pi\in[0,2\pi)\),

\[ \boxed{\;\text{right arc from }\chi_1\text{ to }\chi_2:\ \ R\,[\chi_2-\chi_1]_{2\pi},\qquad \text{left arc: }\ R\,[\chi_1-\chi_2]_{2\pi}\;} \]
Convention hazard. Most robotics code (Shkel & Lumelsky 2001, LaValle 2006, the popular dubins Python package) uses \(x\) east, \(y\) north and \(\theta\) counter-clockwise from \(x\). In that frame the sign of every rotation flips: their "L" is our R if you simply relabel axes. This lesson keeps the course convention throughout — \(n\) first, \(e\) second, angles clockwise from north, \(\operatorname{atan2}(e,n)\) — so that Dubins outputs plug straight into the Part 15 state \((p_n,p_e,\chi)\) without any sign gymnastics.

4. The Six Words: Closed-Form Lengths

Fix \(R=R_{\min}\). From the start pose build \(c_{rs},c_{ls}\) and from the end pose \(c_{re},c_{le}\) with the boxed formulas of Section 3. For a pair of circles write \(d=c_{\text{end}}-c_{\text{start}}\), \(\ell=\|d\|\) and \(\vartheta=\operatorname{atan2}(d_e,d_n)\) (the course from one centre to the other).

4.1 CSC with an external tangent: RSR and LSL

For RSR both tangent points sit at the same polar angle on their circles, \(c_{rs}+R(\sin\chi_t,-\cos\chi_t)\) and \(c_{re}+R(\sin\chi_t,-\cos\chi_t)\), so the straight segment equals \(d\) itself: its length is \(\ell\) and its course is \(\chi_t=\vartheta\). The aircraft turns right from \(\chi_s\) to \(\vartheta\), flies \(\ell\), turns right from \(\vartheta\) to \(\chi_e\):

\[ \boxed{\;L_{RSR}=R[\vartheta-\chi_s]_{2\pi}+\ell+R[\chi_e-\vartheta]_{2\pi},\qquad d=c_{re}-c_{rs}\;} \] \[ \boxed{\;L_{LSL}=R[\chi_s-\vartheta]_{2\pi}+\ell+R[\vartheta-\chi_e]_{2\pi},\qquad d=c_{le}-c_{ls}\;} \]

These two words are always feasible (the external tangent exists for any \(\ell\ge0\)).

4.2 CSC with an internal tangent: RSL and LSR

For RSL the straight segment leaves the right start circle at \(c_{rs}-R\hat n_R(\chi_t)\) and reaches the left end circle at \(c_{le}+R\hat n_R(\chi_t)\), where \(\chi_t\) is the (unknown) course on the segment. Its length \(s\) satisfies

\[ d=c_{le}-c_{rs}=s\,\hat t(\chi_t)+2R\,\hat n_R(\chi_t)\ \ \text{(after moving the two offsets to one side)},\qquad \text{so } \ell^2=s^2+4R^2 . \]

More carefully: the exit point is \(c_{rs}-R\hat n_R(\chi_t)\) and the entry point is \(c_{le}+R\hat n_R(\chi_t)\), so the segment vector is \(d+2R\hat n_R=s\,\hat t\), i.e. \(d=s\,\hat t(\chi_t)-2R\,\hat n_R(\chi_t)\). Hence \(s=\sqrt{\ell^2-4R^2}\), feasible only if \(\ell\ge2R\). Since \(-\hat n_R\) is \(\hat t\) rotated counter-clockwise, \(d\) points counter-clockwise of \(\hat t(\chi_t)\) by \(\arctan(2R/s)=\arcsin(2R/\ell)\), so \(\vartheta=\chi_t-\arcsin(2R/\ell)\), i.e. \(\chi_t=\vartheta+\arcsin(2R/\ell)\). For the mirror word LSR the offsets flip sign, \(d=s\,\hat t+2R\,\hat n_R\), and \(\chi_t=\vartheta-\arcsin(2R/\ell)\). Reading off the arcs:

\[ \boxed{\;L_{RSL}=R[\chi_t-\chi_s]_{2\pi}+\sqrt{\ell^2-4R^2}+R[\chi_t-\chi_e]_{2\pi},\quad \chi_t=\vartheta+\arcsin\frac{2R}{\ell},\quad d=c_{le}-c_{rs}\;} \] \[ \boxed{\;L_{LSR}=R[\chi_s-\chi_t]_{2\pi}+\sqrt{\ell^2-4R^2}+R[\chi_e-\chi_t]_{2\pi},\quad \chi_t=\vartheta-\arcsin\frac{2R}{\ell},\quad d=c_{re}-c_{ls}\;} \]
Note. Beard & McLain (2012, Ch. 11) write the same four CSC lengths with \(\arccos(2R/\ell)\) and polar angles \(\chi\pm\pi/2\); since \(\arccos x=\pi/2-\arcsin x\) the expressions are identical. Their flight-management implementation uses only CSC and assumes waypoints at least \(3R\) apart.

4.3 CCC: RLR and LRL

When the two same-sense circles are close (\(\ell\le4R\)) a third circle of the opposite sense can be tangent to both. For RLR its centre \(c_m\) is at distance \(2R\) from both \(c_{rs}\) and \(c_{re}\):

\[ c_m=c_{rs}+2R\,\hat u(\vartheta_1),\qquad \vartheta_1=\vartheta\pm\alpha,\qquad \alpha=\arccos\frac{\ell}{4R},\qquad \hat u(\theta)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta). \]

The tangent points are the midpoints \(p_1=\tfrac12(c_{rs}+c_m)\) and \(p_2=\tfrac12(c_m+c_{re})\). At \(p_1\) the aircraft is on the right circle at polar angle \(\vartheta_1\), so its course is \(\chi_1=\vartheta_1+\pi/2\); at \(p_2\) it is on the end circle at polar angle \(\vartheta_2=\operatorname{atan2}\) of \(c_m-c_{re}\), so \(\chi_2=\vartheta_2+\pi/2\). The middle arc is a left turn from \(\chi_1\) to \(\chi_2\):

\[ \boxed{\;L_{RLR}=R\big([\chi_1-\chi_s]_{2\pi}+[\chi_1-\chi_2]_{2\pi}+[\chi_e-\chi_2]_{2\pi}\big),\qquad \ell=\|c_{re}-c_{rs}\|\le4R\;} \]

The two signs of \(\pm\alpha\) are mirror images of each other across the line of centres and their middle arcs add up to \(2\pi\); by Dubins' theorem only the one whose middle arc exceeds \(\pi\) can be optimal, so an implementation may evaluate both and keep the shorter. LRL is the mirror image: \(d=c_{le}-c_{ls}\), \(\chi_1=\vartheta_1-\pi/2\), \(\chi_2=\vartheta_2-\pi/2\), and the arcs are \(R([\chi_s-\chi_1]_{2\pi}+[\chi_2-\chi_1]_{2\pi}+[\chi_2-\chi_e]_{2\pi})\).

4.4 The algorithm

\[ \boxed{\;\text{word}^\ast=\arg\min_{w\in\{LSL,RSR,LSR,RSL,RLR,LRL\}\ \text{feasible}} L_w,\qquad L^\ast=L_{\text{word}^\ast}\;} \]

Shkel & Lumelsky (2001) showed how to pick the optimal word from the relative pose without evaluating all six; for a swarm planner the brute "compute all six" is simpler, branch-free and just as fast. Always re-validate by propagating the chosen segments with the exact arc/line kinematics and checking the end pose — that catches sign and modulo mistakes that a length formula alone cannot.

6. The 3-D Dubins Airplane

Owen, Beard & McLain (2015) extend the car with altitude and a bounded flight-path angle: \(\dot h=V\sin\gamma\), \(|\gamma|\le\gamma_{\max}\), with the horizontal motion \(V\cos\gamma\,(\cos\chi,\sin\chi)\) (for small \(\gamma\) the horizontal speed is essentially \(V\)). Let \(L_{2D}\) be the Dubins-car length of the horizontal problem and \(\Delta h=h_e-h_s\) the altitude to gain. Three regimes:

  • Low altitude, \(|\Delta h|\le L_{2D}\tan\gamma_{\max}\): fly the 2-D path with constant \(\gamma=\arctan(\Delta h/L_{2D})\). Example: \(\Delta h=50\) m on the 500 m sanity path gives \(\gamma=5.71^\circ\).
  • High altitude, \(|\Delta h|\gt(L_{2D}+2\pi R_{\min})\tan\gamma_{\max}\): the climb needs a path of length \(|\Delta h|/\tan\gamma_{\max}\); add \(k=\lfloor(|\Delta h|/\tan\gamma_{\max}-L_{2D})/(2\pi R_{\min})\rfloor\) full helical turns at the start (or end) circle and then enlarge the helix radius \(R^\ast\ge R_{\min}\) by bisection until the total length is exactly \(|\Delta h|/\tan\gamma_{\max}\) — the aircraft climbs at \(\gamma_{\max}\) all the way. Example (verified): \(L_{2D}=500\) m, \(\Delta h=600\) m, \(\gamma_{\max}=15^\circ\): required length \(600/\tan15^\circ=2239.2\) m; since \((500+628.3)\tan15^\circ=302.3\) m \(\lt600\) m this is the high case; \(k=\lfloor1739.2/628.3\rfloor=2\) turns and \(R^\ast=1739.2/(4\pi)=138.4\) m (here \(L_{2D}\) does not depend on \(R\), so no bisection is needed).
  • Medium altitude, in between: a full turn would overshoot the altitude, so an intermediate arc of angle \(\varphi\) is inserted next to the first (or last) turn and \(\varphi\) is found by bisection so that the lengthened path climbs exactly at \(\gamma_{\max}\).
Interpretation: altitude never shortens a Dubins path — it can only demand extra length, which is bought with spirals. In a swarm, altitude changes are therefore scheduled where the horizontal path is long anyway, and the extra spiral turns are costed like any other arc.

7. Dubins Paths in the Swarm

7.1 Rendezvous with simultaneous arrival

Three UAVs with speed envelope \(V\in[18,28]\) m/s and \(\phi_{\max}=45^\circ\) must arrive at the pose \((800,400,90^\circ)\) at the same instant. The path is planned with the radius at \(V_{\max}\), \(R=28^2/(g\tan45^\circ)=79.9\) m, so it stays flyable at any speed in the envelope. Verified results:

UAVStart poseWord\(L_i\) (m)Arrival window \([L_i/V_{\max},\,L_i/V_{\min}]\) (s)
1(0, 0, 0°)RSR913.6[32.6, 50.8]
2(−200, 600, 90°)LSR1166.9[41.7, 64.8]
3(400, 900, 180°)RSR904.7[32.3, 50.3]

The windows intersect in \([41.7,\,50.3]\) s, so a common arrival time exists. Choosing the earliest, \(T^\ast=41.7\) s, requires speeds \(L_i/T^\ast=21.9,\ 28.0,\ 21.7\) m/s — UAV 2 flies flat out and the others slow down. This is McLain & Beard's (2005) coordination variable (arrival time) with coordination functions (each UAV's feasible window); Part 19 makes the agreement on \(T^\ast\) distributed. If the windows did not intersect, the alternative is to lengthen the short paths: at a common 23 m/s the mission would take \(1166.9/23=50.7\) s and UAVs 1 and 3 would need an extra \(253.3\) m and \(262.2\) m — a loiter arc or a lateral dog-leg inserted into their Dubins path. Speed changes are cheap and continuous; path lengthening is discrete but works when the speed envelope is narrow (high-speed aircraft, Part 16).

7.2 Other uses

  • Formation assembly: after launch each UAV flies a Dubins path from its pose to its assigned slot pose \((p_i^d,\chi_c)\) (Part 10); the slot assignment itself uses the Dubins lengths as costs in the Hungarian method or CBBA (Part 22) — Euclidean distance underestimates the cost of a slot that is behind the aircraft.
  • Task transitions: leaving an orbit for a new target pose, re-joining a path after an avoidance manoeuvre (Part 20).
  • Sampling-based planning: RRT / RRT\(^\ast\) with Dubins primitives as the steering function (Karaman & Frazzoli 2011) for obstacle-rich airspace.
  • MPC warm starts: the Dubins word gives a feasible initial guess (bang–zero–bang turn rate) for the optimiser of Part 21.

8. Fixed-Wing Interpretation and the High-Speed Note

The radius that enters every formula is the Part 16 minimum radius, and it grows with the square of speed (verified):

High-speed note. Scaling the speed by 6 (25 to 150 m/s) scales every arc by 36. Two consequences for the swarm. (i) Poses that were "far apart" (\(\ell\gg4R\), CSC regime, path length \(\approx\) distance) become "close" (\(\ell\lt4R\)): a formation slot 1 km away at 150 m/s is inside the turning circle, and reaching it costs a CCC manoeuvre of several kilometres. Plan transitions early, and prefer slot changes that are mostly along-track (Part 19). (ii) Latency moves the start pose: at 200 m/s a 0.5 s planning delay displaces it by 100 m, so plan from the predicted pose at the time the path becomes active. Also, because \(R_{\min}\propto V_g^2\), a path planned at cruise speed is unflyable if the aircraft accelerates — plan at \(V_{\max}\) of the envelope, as in Section 7.1.

Wind. The Dubins car lives in still air. In a uniform wind \(w\) the air-relative path is still Dubins, but the ground track of a constant-rate turn is a trochoid, not a circle (McGee & Hedrick 2007; Techy & Woolsey 2009 give the minimum-time solution). A practical approximation: solve the still-air problem to a virtual goal \(p_e-wT\) and iterate on the arrival time \(T\) until it is consistent with the path length (McGee, Spry & Hedrick 2005). Either way the autopilot follows the resulting path in course, not heading (Part 18).

Roll dynamics. A Dubins path has discontinuous curvature at every segment boundary, which asks for an infinite roll rate. With a roll-rate limit \(p_{\max}\) (Part 16) the aircraft lags the path by roughly the time to reach \(\phi_{\max}\); either plan with a slightly larger radius than \(R_{\min}\) (\(R=1.1\)–\(1.3R_{\min}\) is common) or let the path follower absorb the error.

Toward the CLAW This lesson supplies the transition-path generator of the unified law. It consumes the aircraft's (predicted) pose \((p_n,p_e,\chi)\), a target pose delivered by the formation/task layer (a slot \((p_i^d,\chi_c)\), a rendezvous pose, or the entry point of a new path) and the envelope-derived radius \(R=V_{\max}^2/(g\tan\phi_{\max})\). It produces (a) a flyable path — a list of (type, length) segments with their circle centres — handed to the path-following block of Part 18, and (b) its length \(L\), which feeds the timing block of Part 19 (arrival windows \([L/V_{\max},L/V_{\min}]\)) and the cost matrix of Part 22.

9. Implementation

Complete NumPy implementation of all six words in the course convention, with an exact segment propagator used to verify every end pose. Running it reproduces the sanity case and cases A and B of Section 5. 

import numpy as np
TWO_PI = 2 * np.pi

def wrap2pi(a):                       # x mod 2*pi in [0, 2*pi), tolerant of -1e-17
    a = np.mod(a, TWO_PI); return 0.0 if a > TWO_PI - 1e-9 else a
def unit(th): return np.array([np.cos(th), np.sin(th)])
def nR(chi):                          # right-hand normal of the course, NED (n, e)
    return np.array([-np.sin(chi), np.cos(chi)])
def pol(d):                           # length and course (clockwise from north) of a vector
    return np.linalg.norm(d), np.arctan2(d[1], d[0])

def dubins_words(ps, chis, pe, chie, R):
    """All feasible Dubins words: {word: (length, [(type, seg_length), ...])}."""
    ps, pe = np.asarray(ps, float), np.asarray(pe, float)
    crs, cls = ps + R * nR(chis), ps - R * nR(chis)      # start circles
    cre, cle = pe + R * nR(chie), pe - R * nR(chie)      # end circles
    out = {}
    l, th = pol(cre - crs); a1, a3 = wrap2pi(th - chis), wrap2pi(chie - th)      # RSR
    out['RSR'] = (R * (a1 + a3) + l, [('R', R * a1), ('S', l), ('R', R * a3)])
    l, th = pol(cle - cls); a1, a3 = wrap2pi(chis - th), wrap2pi(th - chie)      # LSL
    out['LSL'] = (R * (a1 + a3) + l, [('L', R * a1), ('S', l), ('L', R * a3)])
    l, th = pol(cle - crs)                                                        # RSL
    if l >= 2 * R:
        chit, s = th + np.arcsin(2 * R / l), np.sqrt(l * l - 4 * R * R)
        a1, a3 = wrap2pi(chit - chis), wrap2pi(chit - chie)
        out['RSL'] = (R * (a1 + a3) + s, [('R', R * a1), ('S', s), ('L', R * a3)])
    l, th = pol(cre - cls)                                                        # LSR
    if l >= 2 * R:
        chit, s = th - np.arcsin(2 * R / l), np.sqrt(l * l - 4 * R * R)
        a1, a3 = wrap2pi(chis - chit), wrap2pi(chie - chit)
        out['LSR'] = (R * (a1 + a3) + s, [('L', R * a1), ('S', s), ('R', R * a3)])
    for word, c1, c3, sg in (('RLR', crs, cre, +1), ('LRL', cls, cle, -1)):      # CCC
        l, th = pol(c3 - c1)
        if l > 4 * R: continue
        alpha, best = np.arccos(l / (4 * R)), None
        for sig in (+1, -1):                      # the two tangent middle circles
            th1 = th + sig * alpha; cm = c1 + 2 * R * unit(th1)
            chi1 = th1 + sg * np.pi / 2; chi2 = pol(cm - c3)[1] + sg * np.pi / 2
            a1, a2, a3 = (wrap2pi(sg * (chi1 - chis)), wrap2pi(sg * (chi1 - chi2)),
                          wrap2pi(sg * (chie - chi2)))
            L = R * (a1 + a2 + a3)
            if best is None or L < best[0]:
                best = (L, [(word[0], R * a1), (word[1], R * a2), (word[2], R * a3)])
        out[word] = best
    return out

def dubins_shortest(ps, chis, pe, chie, R):
    w = dubins_words(ps, chis, pe, chie, R); k = min(w, key=lambda q: w[q][0])
    return k, w[k][0], w[k][1]

def step(p, chi, typ, s, R):          # exact propagation along one segment
    if typ == 'S': return p + s * unit(chi), chi
    sg = 1 if typ == 'R' else -1; c = p + sg * R * nR(chi); chi2 = chi + sg * s / R
    return c - sg * R * nR(chi2), chi2

def end_pose(ps, chis, segs, R):
    p, chi = np.asarray(ps, float), chis
    for typ, s in segs: p, chi = step(p, chi, typ, s, R)
    return p, chi

10. Common Pitfalls

  • Axis convention. Porting an ENU/counter-clockwise implementation into NED swaps the meaning of L and R and the sign in \(\hat n_R\). Validate with the heading-north test: the right circle must be east of the aircraft.
  • Modulo at zero. \(\operatorname{mod}(-10^{-17},2\pi)=2\pi\) turns a zero-length arc into a full circle (we saw \(1128\) m instead of \(500\) m before adding the tolerance in wrap2pi).
  • Radius at the wrong speed. \(R_{\min}\propto V_g^2\); a path planned at cruise is unflyable after acceleration. Plan at \(V_{\max}\) when the speed will be used for timing.
  • Skipping feasibility tests. Internal tangents need \(\ell\ge2R\), CCC words need \(\ell\le4R\); evaluating the formulas outside their domain gives NaN or, worse, a plausible wrong number.
  • Assuming CSC only. Fine for waypoints more than \(4R\) apart; at high speed \(4R\) is kilometres, so CCC cases are common for slot changes (case B, case C).
  • Heading versus course in wind. The Dubins geometry is an air-mass construction; follow it in course and let the wind triangle (Part 15) supply the heading, or plan with trochoids.
  • Curvature discontinuities. The roll loop cannot jump to \(\phi_{\max}\); inflate \(R\) or expect cross-track error at every junction.
  • Stale start pose. Plan from the pose predicted at activation time; at 200 m/s every 100 ms of latency is 20 m.

11. Exercises

Exercise 1 (derivation). Starting from \(\hat t(\chi)=(\cos\chi,\sin\chi)\) in (north, east) coordinates, show that the centre of the right turning circle is \(p+R(-\sin\chi,\cos\chi)\), and verify it for an aircraft heading east (\(\chi=90^\circ\)).
Show solution
A clockwise rotation by \(90^\circ\) maps \(\chi\mapsto\chi+\pi/2\), so the right normal is \((\cos(\chi+\pi/2),\sin(\chi+\pi/2))=(-\sin\chi,\cos\chi)\). For \(\chi=90^\circ\) this is \((-1,0)\): the centre is \(R\) to the south — correct, since the right-hand side of an east-bound aircraft is south.
Exercise 2 (numeric). Recompute \(L_{RSR}\) for case A by hand from the centres \(c_{rs}=(0,100)\) and \(c_{re}=(200,400)\).
Show solution
\(d=(200,300)\), \(\ell=\sqrt{200^2+300^2}=360.6\) m, \(\vartheta=\operatorname{atan2}(300,200)=0.9828\) rad \(=56.3^\circ\). Arcs: \(100\times0.9828=98.3\) m and \(100\times(\pi/2-0.9828)=58.8\) m. Total \(98.3+360.6+58.8=517.6\) m, as in the table.
Exercise 3 (derivation). Prove that the internal tangent segment of RSL has length \(\sqrt{\ell^2-4R^2}\) and explain why RSL is infeasible for \(\ell\lt2R\).
Show solution
The segment direction \(\hat t\) and the right normal \(\hat n_R\) are orthonormal, and \(d=s\hat t+2R\hat n_R\) (the two radius offsets point the same way because the circles have opposite sense). Hence \(\ell^2=s^2+4R^2\). For \(\ell\lt2R\) the circles overlap and no line can be tangent to both from opposite sides.
Exercise 4 (numeric, high speed). An aircraft at 150 m/s with \(\phi_{\max}=45^\circ\) must reverse course (goal directly behind, same track). What is the Dubins length, and what is it at 25 m/s?
Show solution
\(R_{\min}=150^2/(9.81\tan45^\circ)=2293.6\) m; a reversal with the goal \(2R\) abeam is a half circle, \(\pi R_{\min}=7205\) m. At 25 m/s, \(R_{\min}=63.7\) m and the reversal is \(200\) m — 36 times shorter.
Exercise 5 (numeric). For the rendezvous of Section 7.1, what common arrival time results if the envelope is narrowed to \([22,26]\) m/s? Is it feasible?
Show solution
Windows: UAV 1 \([913.6/26,913.6/22]=[35.1,41.5]\) s, UAV 2 \([44.9,53.0]\) s, UAV 3 \([34.8,41.1]\) s. The intersection is empty (UAV 2 cannot arrive before 44.9 s, the others cannot arrive after 41.5 s): speed alone cannot coordinate; lengthen the short paths so that they too need 44.9 s at \(V_{\max}\), i.e. by at least \(1166.9-913.6=253.3\) m (UAV 1) and \(1166.9-904.7=262.2\) m (UAV 3).
Exercise 6 (code). Add to dubins_shortest a sampler that returns the path as \(N\) poses, and use it to compute the ground track in a wind of 5 m/s from the west by adding \(w\,t\) with \(t=s/V\). Observe that the arcs become trochoids.
Show solution
Sample each segment with step(p, chi, typ, s*k/n, R) for \(k=0..n\), accumulate the arc length \(s\) and add \((w_n,w_e)\,s/V\) to each sampled position. With \(w=(0,5)\) m/s and \(V=20\) m/s, every point drifts east by \(0.25\) m per metre flown; circles stretch into trochoids and the end pose misses the goal by \(0.25L\) m east, which is why wind-aware planning moves the goal upwind.

12. Key Takeaways & Formula Sheet

  • Dubins car in NED: \(\dot p_n=V\cos\chi,\ \dot p_e=V\sin\chi,\ \dot\chi=u,\ |u|\le V/R_{\min}\), \(\chi\) clockwise from north.
  • Turning centres: \(c_R=p+R(-\sin\chi,\cos\chi)\), \(c_L=p-R(-\sin\chi,\cos\chi)\); polar angle \(\vartheta=\chi\mp\pi/2\) on the right/left circle.
  • Arcs: right \(R[\chi_2-\chi_1]_{2\pi}\), left \(R[\chi_1-\chi_2]_{2\pi}\).
  • External tangent (RSR/LSL): segment \(=d\), course \(\vartheta\), always feasible. Internal tangent (RSL/LSR): \(s=\sqrt{\ell^2-4R^2}\), \(\chi_t=\vartheta\pm\arcsin(2R/\ell)\), needs \(\ell\ge2R\).
  • CCC (RLR/LRL): \(c_m=c_1+2R\hat u(\vartheta\pm\arccos(\ell/4R))\), needs \(\ell\le4R\); optimal middle arc \(\gt\pi\).
  • Planner: \(L^\ast=\min_w L_w\) over the six words; verify by exact propagation.
  • Dubins airplane: low / medium / high altitude regimes; extra length bought with spirals of radius \(R^\ast\ge R_{\min}\).
  • Swarm: arrival window \([L_i/V_{\max},L_i/V_{\min}]\); common \(T^\ast\) exists iff \(\max_iL_i/V_{\max}\le\min_iL_i/V_{\min}\); otherwise lengthen paths.
  • High speed: \(R_{\min}\propto V_g^2\) (36× from 25 to 150 m/s); plan at \(V_{\max}\), from the predicted pose, in course not heading.
